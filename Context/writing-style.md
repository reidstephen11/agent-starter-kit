## Student-facing writing

<!-- ASK NOW
     Q: "When you write instructions for your students — how should that sound? Think of
         how you'd word a task sheet for the class you described."
     Eg: "Short sentences, second person, one instruction per line. Numbered steps for
         anything procedural. I never use 'simply' or 'just' — if it were simple they
         wouldn't need the sheet. Reading age a bit below year level, because the reading
         shouldn't be the hard part unless I meant it to be."
     Probe once if the answer is only a tone word like "friendly": ask for one thing they
     always do and one thing they never do. -->

## Language

<!-- ASK LATER

## Documents that argue a position

<!-- ASK LATER. This is where AI writing fails most visibly: it defaults to persuasive
     marketing prose, which a sceptical colleague reads as spin and dismisses. If the
     teacher has no preference, record the default below rather than leaving it empty.
     Q: "Do you write proposals, rationales or briefing notes that someone might disagree
         with? If so, how should those read?"
     Eg: "Plain and analytical. If I'm asking for something I say what it costs and what it
         doesn't fix, because the first question I'll get is the one I didn't answer."

     Default, if the teacher has no strong view:
     - Plain and analytical, never persuasive. No flourish where a plain statement carries
       the same information.
     - No slogans as argument, no false binaries, no manufactured urgency.
     - Claim only what the evidence carries; attribute contested claims to a named source.
     - Name the limits in the text — say what the proposal does not solve.
     - "Proposed" and "would" while it is a proposal; "will" only once it's decided.

     The test: could a sceptical reader argue with this, or only agree or disbelieve it? -->
